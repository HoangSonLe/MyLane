# Brain Reset Story — Research Basis

## Purpose

This note records the evidence used to shape the five-chapter Brain Reset story. It supports editorial accuracy; it does not turn My Lane into a medical or educational product.

The story must remain friendly, short, and grounded in ordinary consequences. It must not diagnose the player, promise recovery, or claim that playing a memory game treats a health condition.

## Claim Boundaries

- “Brain rot” is a cultural and internet term, not a medical diagnosis.
- Research about short-form video, social media, electronic-media use, attention, and sleep often reports associations. The story must not silently rewrite those associations as universal causation.
- A short My Lane session is framed as deliberate practice with one clear task. It is not framed as treatment, prevention, or proof of cognitive improvement.
- The reset also includes practical boundaries: fewer interruptions, moving the phone away from bed, and doing one task at a time.

## Sources and Story Use

### Definition and cultural context

[Oxford University Press — “Brain rot” named Oxford Word of the Year 2024](https://corp.oup.com/news/brain-rot-named-oxford-word-of-the-year-2024/)

- Supports the definition of “brain rot” as a supposed decline associated with overconsuming trivial or unchallenging content.
- Supports treating the phrase as cultural language about virtual life, not a clinical label.
- Used in Chapter 1.

### Interrupted intentions

[Chiossi et al. — Short-Form Videos Degrade Our Capacity to Retain Intentions](https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3580778), CHI 2023.

- The experiment examined how short-form video interruptions and rapid context switching affected prospective memory: remembering to carry out an intention after an interruption.
- Used to ground the forgotten-work-task scene in Chapter 2.
- The story does not generalize this result into a claim that every short video damages memory.

### Attention and inhibitory control

[Nguyen et al. — Feeds, Feelings, and Focus](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000498), *Psychological Bulletin*, 2025.

- This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized 71 studies with 98,299 participants.
- Greater short-form video use was associated with poorer cognitive outcomes, with the strongest relationships reported for attention and inhibitory control.
- Used to ground the divided-attention consequences in Chapter 3.
- The story describes an association and preserves uncertainty about cause, individual differences, and direction of effect.

### Sleep

[Electronic Media Use and Sleep Quality: Updated Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11077410/), 2024.

- The review found that both general and problematic electronic-media use were associated with more sleep problems across the included studies.
- The authors note substantial heterogeneity and that correlational evidence cannot establish direction of causation.
- Used to ground the late-night scrolling and next-morning fatigue scenes in Chapters 1 and 3.

### Practical digital boundaries

[American Psychological Association — Connected and content: Managing healthy technology use](https://www.apa.org/topics/social-media-internet/healthy-technology-use)

- Supports practical actions such as turning off unnecessary notifications and avoiding stimulating phone use near bedtime.
- Used to shape An’s non-dramatic reset in Chapters 4 and 5.
